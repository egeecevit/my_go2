Shorter Than One Stance

The MPC looks a tenth of a second ahead against a quarter-second stance, so it never sees the next touchdown. Everything says that is too short. With the step count made a run-time parameter, so lookahead could finally be varied without also coarsening the contact grid, the honest answer is that lengthening it makes this controller five times worse — and just as much so on a perfect state estimate. The horizon is not the problem. The reference it is asked to track across that horizon is.

The question, and a correction on the way to it

The robot falls out of a 0.55 m/s trot after 20–35 s, and the leading structural suspect was the prediction horizon: ten steps at mpc.dt = 0.01 is 0.1 s against a 0.25 s stance, so a solve never contains a contact change and the force redistribution across the diagonal swap is never planned, only reacted to.

I could not test that, and I reported the result as though I had. The step count was static constexpr int HORIZON = 10, so the only way to lengthen the lookahead was to raise dt — which is also the grid the contact schedule is sampled on. MdlTrot builds it at tk = elapsed + k·dt, so at dt = 0.04 a 250 ms stance is six points and each touchdown is quantized to a 40 ms cell. Every cell in the table below varies lookahead and contact-timing resolution together. It cannot separate them.

What survives: 0.01 is the best dt of those tried, which is a real and useful result about resolution. What does not survive: anything about whether a longer horizon helps. The reasoning that a trot controller ought to see both diagonal stances — a 0.5 s lookahead, or 50 steps at dt = 0.01 — is sound, and the code simply could not express it at any setting.

horizon is now a run-time parameter alongside dt and solve_period, and the question has been asked properly: hold dt at 0.01, hold solve_period at 0.01, vary only the step count. Lookahead really does hurt — but for a reason that is about the cost, not about prediction.

A trot cannot hold still, and the reference insists that it does. MdlTrot fills reference[k] with roll = pitch = 0, a constant height and a constant velocity at every horizon step, weighted 1 / 1 / 50 / 1. Measured on ground truth while walking cleanly, the trunk swings roll ±26 mrad, pitch ±22 mrad about a nose-up 16.6 mrad mean, and vertical velocity ±100 mm/s, all at gait frequency.

At ten steps the solver sees a fifth of a gait cycle and is mostly making a correction. At twenty-five it sees half a cycle of the gait's own motion laid out in front of it and spends ground reaction force cancelling it, with twenty-four unreachable future steps outvoting the one interval actually applied. Torque climbing and the trunk sinking as the horizon grows is what that looks like from outside.

So the fix is a gait-consistent reference, not a longer horizon and not a weight. Until reference[k] carries the body motion a trot actually produces, extra lookahead is extra disagreement, and the shortest horizon that still stabilises wins by default.

How a fall registers

The gait schedule stops early only because MdlTrot aborted, and it aborts at the bottom of a topple: the trunk sinks, a leg reaches its limit, and the Cartesian force command is rejected three cycles running. Body height confirms it in every case — a typical failure runs 0.29 → 0.27 → 0.20 → 0.10 m before the rejection. Nothing went non-finite and no QP failed to solve, so these are genuine falls and not a numerical abort.

02

mpc.dt was three parameters wearing one name

Horizon step, solve interval, and contact-timing resolution.

MdlTrot re-solved every mpc.dt seconds, so raising the horizon step from 0.01 to 0.04 also dropped the solver from 100 Hz to 25 Hz. A single sweep of dt moves both and can only report their sum. That is what mpc.solve_period is for: the horizon step now sets the lookahead and nothing else, and the solve interval is its own key, defaulting to dt so every existing config behaves exactly as it did.

That split was worth having, and it was not enough. The third role stayed hidden: dt is also the grid the contact schedule is sampled on, and the step count was a compile-time constant, so lookahead could still only be bought with resolution. Three keys now, and one rule between them

horizon is the step count, dt the discretization interval, solve_period the re-solve interval; lookahead is horizon × dt. Keep solve_period ≤ dt: the QP plans the first force to act for one dt and then be replaced, so re-solving sooner just discards the plan early, while re-solving later holds it past its allotted interval for a successor that never arrives — a degradation with nothing to do with the horizon.

Height in metres, torque in N·m. The short horizon holds the trunk flat at 0.291–0.293 m on 4–5 N·m. The long ones climb to 0.305 m on rising torque with peaks near twice as high, and go down shortly after.

The likely reason is that the cost has no terminal term. Over ten steps of 0.04 s the solver can accept large error early and promise to recover it late, and the force it commits for the one interval actually applied is correspondingly soft; the height weight of 50 then has to make that up. This is a hypothesis consistent with the traces, not something the sweep proves — it would be tested by adding a terminal weight, which nothing here has tried.

04

The estimator's drift is not in the loop

Horizontal position carries zero weight. It cannot reach the controller.

state_weights = [1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 50, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1] in the order roll pitch yaw / x y z / wx wy wz / vx vy vz. The solver never sees a horizontal position error. In an 87 s run the estimate ended 2.1 m out in x and 3.4 m out in y and none of it could reach the controller. On flat ground the robot genuinely does not care where it thinks it is, and position_reference_clamp is inert at this tuning.

The last two are one thing. v_err_y tracks v_x · δψ to within a few mm/s for eighty seconds — the estimator's world frame is simply rotated by the unobservable yaw drift. And that rotation cancels where it matters: the stance sweep is built on _vfilt = LPF(_Rbwᵀ · _comVel), where δψ appears in both factors and divides out. The flat body-frame velocity error above is that cancellation, measured while δψ nearly doubled.

Why stopping and restarting looks like a reset

Everything a restart clears is controller state; everything that carries over is estimator state, and the estimator's carried-over quantities are the zero-weight ones. _trotEntry() reseeds the gait phase datum, _vfilt, _desPos including the height datum, _desYaw and the speed ramp, and releasing the solver drops its warm start. δψ and the position drift pass straight through untouched — δψ kept growing through the stop, 57 to 87 mrad while the robot stood still. The estimates were not corrected. They did not need to be, and that a restart helps is itself evidence the problem is on the controller side.

05

What is left

The horizon is not eliminated — it was never fairly tested.
largest in-loop error

The −50 mm/s vertical velocity bias, against a height weight of 50. It parks the trunk about 25 mm above its standing height for the whole trot and roughly doubles roll amplitude — 60–90 mrad per cycle against 25–47 on ground truth — from the first stride, constant rather than accumulating. That is the whole of what the estimator costs, and it is the difference between 67 s and 25–33 s. Deliberately not touched here so it would not confound the sweep.

what would make lookahead pay

A gait-consistent reference[k]. The schedule already knows what the body will be doing — MdlTrot has the phase, the duty and the stance velocity in hand when it fills the horizon — so the roll, pitch, height and vertical velocity a trot produces could be written into the reference instead of zeros. Then a long horizon predicts the gait rather than arguing with it. This is the change the measurement points at, and it is real work in _solveMPC, not a knob.

tested, dead

The input cost. Raising force_weight from 1e-6 was the obvious way to stop the solver spending force on an unreachable target, and there is no regime where it works: 1e-4 changes nothing, and 1e-3 and 1e-2 stop the solver pushing at all — the robot sags to 0.16–0.24 m and makes no headway. Worth knowing that a collapse is not a fall, so it scores as a survival unless the metric checks that the robot actually walked.

parked

Duty factor. duty = 0.5 is a pure trot with no double support, so there is never an interval with more than two feet down and the solver never has a support polygon to redistribute over — only a line. trot.toml already suggests 0.6 as the first thing to try when the robot is unsteady.

Forty-five headless runs, MuJoCo, Go2 model, forward_velocity = 0.55, period = 0.5, duty = 0.5. Seeds vary orientationestimator.simnoise.seed only; ground-truth rows set use_ground_truth on both estimator stages. A run counts as a fall when MdlTrot aborts, which it does at the bottom of a topple, and separately as a collapse when it never aborts but never walks. The horizon = 50 runs are driven by supervisor.exit_time rather than a wall-clock budget, because a 600-variable QP runs the simulator about fifteen times slower than real time and a wall budget would have bought five seconds of trotting and called it a survival.
